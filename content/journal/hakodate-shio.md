Hold a spoonful of Hakodate shio ramen up to the light and you can almost read through it. The broth is a pale gold, clear enough to see the noodles lying straight beneath the surface. In a region famous for hearty, warming bowls, Hakodate's ramen is a quiet surprise: light, clean and seasoned with little more than salt.

## A harbour that opened early

Hakodate sits at the southern tip of Hokkaido, facing the strait that separates Japan's northern island from Honshu. In 1859 it became one of the first Japanese ports opened to foreign trade, and the city still wears that history openly. Western-style churches and old brick warehouses sit on its slopes and along its waterfront, and the star-shaped Goryōkaku fort is one of its best-known sights. Its morning market and the night view from Mount Hakodate draw visitors from all over Japan.

That outward-looking past is part of the city's personality. Local lore often links the early days of its noodle soups to the treaty-port years and the Chinese cooks who arrived with them. Whatever the precise story, shio ramen has long been the bowl Hakodate calls its own.

Within Hokkaido, it's often grouped with two other regional styles: [Sapporo's miso ramen](/journal/sapporo-miso-ramen/) and [Asahikawa's shoyu ramen](/journal/asahikawa-shoyu/). Three cities, three seasonings, one island. I love how neatly they divide the map, and how differently each one answers the same long northern winter.

## What's in the bowl

Shio (塩) simply means salt. In ramen, it describes the tare (タレ), the concentrated seasoning that goes into the bowl before the stock. Soy sauce and miso both bring colour and strong flavours of their own. Salt brings neither, so a shio ramen lives or dies by the quality of the stock it seasons.

In Hakodate, that stock is typically made from chicken or pork bones, simmered gently rather than boiled hard, so it stays clear. Kombu (昆布), the kelp that underpins so much Japanese cooking, often goes in too, adding a soft, rounded savouriness that fills in the space where soy or miso would otherwise be. Hokkaido's coasts are famous for kombu, so it's a natural local partner. There's more on why kelp matters so much in our guide to [kombu](/journal/kombu/).

The noodles are straight and usually on the thinner side, with a smooth surface that lets the broth glide along them. Toppings tend to be simple: a few slices of chashu (チャーシュー, braised pork), menma (メンマ, seasoned bamboo shoots), chopped green onion, and sometimes a little greenery or a sheet of nori. Nothing here is meant to hide the soup.

The flavour is gentle but never thin. A good shio broth has a clean saltiness at the front, a savoury depth that builds as you eat, and a lightness that makes it easy to finish. I think it is the ramen that most rewards slowing down.

## How to eat it

- **Start with the soup.** Before touching the noodles, take a sip or two of broth on its own. With shio ramen, that first taste tells you most of what you need to know.
- **Try it plain first.** If there's pepper or chilli on the counter, hold off until you've had a few mouthfuls. It's very easy to drown a delicate broth.
- **Keep a steady pace.** Thinner noodles soften as they sit, and a clear broth shows every change in texture.
- **Watch the colour.** As you eat, notice how the broth stays bright and clear right to the bottom. That clarity is the cook's work, and it's worth a moment's appreciation.
- **Pair it with the city.** Hakodate is famous for its seafood, especially squid, and many visitors build a morning around the market. A light bowl of shio ramen fits neatly into a day of grazing, where a heavier bowl might not.

## The confidence of a clear soup

There's something brave about a bowl you can see straight through. Richer styles can hide a great deal behind fat, spice or a strong tare. A clear shio broth has nowhere to hide, which is why I think it says more about a cook than almost any other kind of ramen.

Hakodate's version also says something about its city. A port that spent so long looking outwards, receiving ships, people and new ideas, ended up with a bowl that is open, clear and unpretentious. It doesn't try to warm you with sheer weight, as so many northern dishes do. It warms you with care.

On a cold evening in Hokkaido, you might expect the richest bowl to stay with you longest. Don't be surprised if it's the lightest one you remember.
