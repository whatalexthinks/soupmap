Some noodles are built for chewing. Kishimen is built for gliding.

*Kishimen* (きしめん) is Nagoya's own noodle: flat, wide and thin, like a ribbon of fresh pasta cut a little too generously. It slips off the chopsticks, slides across the tongue and is gone almost before you've noticed. I think of it as the most graceful member of the udon family.

## Ribbon, not rope

Kishimen is made from the same basic dough as udon, wheat flour, water and salt, but it's rolled out thin and cut wide. The result is a flat strip with a smooth, almost silky surface and a gentle chew. Because it's so thin, it cooks quickly, which matters in a city where plenty of kishimen is eaten in a hurry.

That broad, flat shape also changes how the bowl tastes. There's more surface for the broth to cling to, so each mouthful carries more soup than a round noodle would. Some lists of Japan's [three great udon](/journal/three-great-udon/) put kishimen in the disputed third slot, and I can see why.

Nagoya is famous for bold food: *miso katsu* (pork cutlet under a thick miso sauce), peppery *tebasaki* chicken wings, grilled eel over rice in the *hitsumabushi* style, and *miso nikomi udon* (味噌煮込みうどん), firm udon simmered in a clay pot of the region's dark soybean miso. Against that backdrop, kishimen is the gentle one.

## The bowl, from the bottom up

A classic bowl of hot kishimen is simple, and every part has a job.

- **The broth** is a hot soy and dashi soup, savoury and fragrant with fish.
- **The katsuobushi** (鰹節) goes on top in a generous heap. These are shavings of dried, smoked skipjack tuna, and in the steam they curl and flutter as if they were alive. Our article on [katsuobushi](/journal/katsuobushi/) explains how it's made.
- **The greens** are usually a leafy vegetable such as *komatsuna* (小松菜), a mild Japanese mustard spinach, for freshness and a flash of colour.
- **The extras** often include a slice of fried tofu or fish cake.

In summer, many places also serve kishimen cold, with chilled broth or a dipping sauce. The flat noodle is lovely this way: the cold firms it up a little and makes its smoothness even more obvious.

## Platform noodles

Kishimen's most famous stage might be Nagoya Station itself. Standing noodle counters on the platforms, including those of the Tōkaidō Shinkansen, serve kishimen to commuters and travellers between trains. You order, the bowl arrives in moments, and you eat standing at the counter while the announcements drift past.

It's quick food, but I don't think it's lesser food. There's something deeply satisfying about a noodle that suits its setting so well: thin enough to cook fast, light enough to eat on your feet, warm enough to make a cold platform feel friendly. Standing counters like these often take orders through a ticket machine, and if you've never used one, our guide to the [ticket machine](/journal/ticket-machine/) will help.

One practical note: a Shinkansen only pauses at Nagoya for a short time, so platform kishimen is for when you're changing trains or starting a journey there, not for dashing out during a stop.

## A name nobody can agree on

Where does the word "kishimen" come from? Nobody knows for sure, and the theories are half the fun.

- One links it to *Kishū*, the old province that is now Wakayama, suggesting the noodle or its makers came from there.
- Another points to *kiji*, pheasant, imagining an early version topped with pheasant meat.
- A third connects it to an old flour dish written 棊子麺, whose dough was cut into small shapes said to resemble the stones used in the board game Go.

None of these is proven, and I'm happy to leave it that way. A noodle this easygoing doesn't need a pedigree.

## How to eat it

- **Start with the flakes.** Taste the katsuobushi while it's still crisp and smoky, then let the rest sink and soften into the broth.
- **Lift gently.** The flat noodles are slippery. Hold the bowl close and let them slide.
- **Slurp freely.** It cools the noodles and carries the aroma.
- **Add a pinch of shichimi** (七味), the seven-spice chilli blend, if you like a little heat.
- **Try the contrast.** Eat kishimen one day and miso nikomi udon the next, and you'll understand both sides of Nagoya's noodle personality.

I think kishimen gets overlooked because it's so easy to eat. But ease is its own kind of craft. A noodle that cooks in moments, carries its broth perfectly and suits a train platform as well as a dining table is a noodle that knows exactly what it's for.
