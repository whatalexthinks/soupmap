A good bowl of tantanmen (担々麺) is easy to spot. The broth is a warm, opaque orange, somewhere between terracotta and sunset, with a ring of red chilli oil glinting around the edge. In the middle sits a mound of minced pork and, often, a few bright green leaves of bok choy. It smells of toasted sesame and gentle heat, and it looks like comfort.

What makes tantanmen fascinating is that the dish it's named after doesn't usually look like this at all.

## Street noodles from Sichuan

Tantanmen is the Japanese name for dandan noodles (担担面), a classic of Sichuan in southwestern China. The name comes from the shoulder pole that street vendors once used to carry their noodles and cooking gear around town: *dan* means to carry a load on a pole across the shoulders. These were quick, cheap snacks, served in small portions to people on the move.

Crucially, the original Sichuan dish has little or no soup. The noodles sit in a small amount of intensely flavoured sauce, typically built on chilli oil, Sichuan pepper and soy, and they're topped with savoury minced pork and preserved vegetables. You mix it all together and eat it in a few mouthfuls. It's punchy, numbing and over quickly.

## The Japanese makeover

In Japan, the dish took a different path. Chen Kenmin (陳建民), a Sichuan-born chef who built his career in Japan, is widely credited with adapting dandan noodles into a soup in the 1950s, to suit Japanese diners who were used to eating their noodles in broth. He is also often credited with popularising mapo tofu in Japan, and he was the father of Chen Kenichi, a celebrated chef in his own right.

Japanese tantanmen today is usually built on a chicken or pork stock enriched with sesame paste, neri goma (練り胡麻), until it turns creamy, then sharpened with rayu (ラー油), Japanese-style chilli oil. Minced pork, stir-fried with seasonings, goes on top, along with bok choy or other greens and often some chopped green onion.

Compared with the Sichuan original, it's milder, rounder and much more of a full meal. The heat is still there, but it's cushioned by the nutty sesame, and the numbing Sichuan pepper is often toned down. I think of it as a dish that kept the spirit of the original but changed its shape to fit a new home, much as ramen itself did when Chinese noodle soups arrived in Japan. Our [short history of ramen](/journal/ramen-history/) tells that bigger story.

You'll find tantanmen at Chinese restaurants across Japan and on the menus of many ramen shops, where it sits happily beside the [four classic ramen flavours](/journal/four-ramen-flavours/) as a sesame-rich alternative. On a menu, look for 担々麺 or the variant spelling 坦々麺; both are read the same way.

## Soupless, full circle

In a satisfying twist, Japan has also taken enthusiastically to shirunashi tantanmen (汁なし担々麺), which simply means tantanmen without soup. These bowls lean back towards the Sichuan original: noodles tossed with sesame, chilli oil and minced pork, often with a stronger hit of Sichuan pepper. Hiroshima in particular has become known for its soupless style, with a pronounced numbing kick.

Soupless bowls are meant to be mixed thoroughly, so the sauce at the bottom coats every strand. Some shops offer a little rice at the end to mop up whatever is left, which I think is one of the best ideas in noodle eating.

The two styles feel like different dishes. The soup version is cosy and mellow. The soupless one is sharper, more concentrated and more insistent, closer to the street snack that started it all.

## How to order and eat it

- **Choose your style.** If you want soup, order tantanmen. If you want something closer to Sichuan, look for shirunashi on the menu.
- **Ask about spice.** Many shops let you choose a heat level. If you're unsure, start in the middle; chilli oil builds as you eat.
- **Stir properly.** In a soup bowl, give the minced pork a stir so it seasons the broth. In a soupless bowl, mix from the bottom until every noodle is glossy.
- **Protect your shirt.** Chilli oil is unforgiving. Slurp gently, and accept the paper apron if one is offered.
- **Expect the tingle.** Sichuan pepper doesn't burn so much as buzz. If your lips start to tingle, that's the point, not a problem.

## A dish that keeps moving

Tantanmen is a good reminder that food rarely stays still. A street snack carried on shoulder poles through Sichuan became a creamy Japanese soup, and then Japanese cooks turned around and rediscovered the soupless original, numbing pepper and all. Neither is the "real" version. Both are what happens when a good idea travels and finds new people to feed.

Which do I prefer? It depends on the weather. On a cold day, the sesame soup. On a hot one, the soupless bowl and a tall glass of cold water.
