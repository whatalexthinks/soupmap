Picture a bowl of ramen in a cartoon, or on a shop curtain, and the chances are you're picturing this one. A clear, amber-brown soup. A tangle of curly noodles. A slice of pork, a few strips of bamboo shoot, a sheet of nori and a little white disc with a pink spiral.

That's Tokyo shoyu ramen, often still called by an older name, *chuka soba* (中華そば). It isn't the richest bowl in Japan or the most fashionable. But in many ways it's the one that started everything.

## Shina soba, chuka soba, ramen

Noodle soups arrived in Japan with Chinese cooks and Chinese communities, especially in port cities like Yokohama, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. For a long time the Japanese didn't call the dish ramen at all.

The early name was *shina soba* (支那そば), "Chinese soba", using an old word for China that is now considered offensive and fell out of use after the Second World War. It was largely replaced by *chuka soba* (中華そば), which means the same thing in more neutral terms. The word *ramen* (ラーメン) became the everyday name later, and many people credit instant noodles, such as Nissin's Chicken Ramen of 1958, with helping it stick.

Chuka soba never went away, though. Plenty of shops still use it on their menus, especially for a classic soy bowl, and to many Japanese diners the name carries a warm, nostalgic glow, like calling a café a "tearoom". If you want the bigger story, I've traced it in [the history of ramen](/journal/ramen-history/).

## Rairaiken and the Asakusa start

The shop most often named at the start of Tokyo's ramen story is Rairaiken (来々軒), which opened in Asakusa in 1910. It's frequently called the city's first ramen specialty shop: not the first place in Japan to sell Chinese-style noodle soup, but an early and influential restaurant built around it, in one of Tokyo's busiest entertainment districts. The usual account is that its founder hired cooks from Yokohama's Chinese community to make the noodles and soup.

What it served set the pattern for the Tokyo bowl. Over the following decades, versions of that soy-seasoned noodle soup spread across the city through small shops and street stalls, adapting gradually to Japanese tastes, with more dashi and more soy.

## Anatomy of a classic bowl

A good Tokyo chuka soba is a study in balance. Here's what usually goes into it:

- **The stock** is clear, made from chicken and pork bones, often with *dashi* ingredients such as dried sardines, bonito flakes or kombu. Clarity matters: the bones are simmered gently, not boiled hard, so the soup stays bright.
- **The tare** is soy-based, which gives the soup its colour and a deep, savoury edge. That's what makes this a *shoyu* (醤油) ramen; [the four big flavours](/journal/four-ramen-flavours/) explains how tare and stock fit together.
- **The noodles** are curly and springy, so they catch the soup as you lift them.
- **The toppings** are almost a uniform. *Chashu* (チャーシュー), sliced pork. *Menma* (メンマ), seasoned, fermented bamboo shoots. *Naruto* (なると), a white fish cake with a pink swirl that's widely said to be named after the whirlpools of the Naruto Strait. A sheet of *nori*. And chopped *negi* (ねぎ), green onion.

The first sip should taste of soy and chicken, with a quiet note of the sea. A thin sheen of oil carries the aroma. Nothing shouts. You could happily drink the whole bowl, and many people do.

Some neighbourhoods became especially associated with this style. Ogikubo, in western Tokyo, is one of the names that comes up most often, and "Ogikubo ramen" still suggests a classic, soy-seasoned bowl in the old manner.

## How to order and eat it

Classic chuka soba is about as approachable as ramen gets, which makes it a perfect first bowl in Tokyo.

- **Look for the words.** On a menu or ticket machine, 中華そば or 醤油ラーメン (*shoyu rāmen*) usually points you to the classic.
- **Taste before you season.** Pepper is the traditional table condiment, and a little white pepper suits this soup well. Add it after a few sips, not before.
- **Mind the noodles.** Thin curly noodles soften as they sit, so it pays to eat steadily.
- **Try the wonton version.** Many old-style shops offer *wantanmen* (ワンタン麺), chuka soba with silky dumplings, if you'd like something a little more filling.

You'll also find this style threaded through the city's ramen streets and station basements, and Tokyo's other great bowl, [tsukemen](/journal/tsukemen/), grew up in the same world of soy and dashi. ## Why the old bowl still matters

It's easy to overlook a bowl this familiar in a city full of thrilling new ramen. But I'd argue that Tokyo shoyu is the yardstick everything else is measured against. Every pork-bone cream, every dipping broth, every inventive modern bowl is, in a sense, a reply to that clear brown soup from Asakusa. It's worth tasting the original question before you hear all the answers.
