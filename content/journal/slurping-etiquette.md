Step into a busy ramen shop at lunchtime and listen before you look. Under the hiss of the noodle boilers and the clack of bowls on the counter, there's a steady, unembarrassed sound: people slurping. Loudly, happily, without apology.

If you were raised to eat soup in silence, this can feel like walking into a room where everyone is breaking the same rule at once. Here, it isn't a rule. Ramen manners in Japan are mostly about respect for the cook, the food and the people waiting behind you, and very little about keeping quiet. This is what actually matters.

## Why slurping is the polite choice

In Japan, slurping noodles is normal. It goes for ramen, soba and udon alike, and nobody at the counter will give it a second thought. If anything, a perfectly silent noodle eater is the one who stands out.

There are good practical reasons for it. Ramen arrives very hot, and drawing air in with the noodles cools each mouthful on its way. It also carries the aroma of the broth up with it, so you smell what you're tasting. And it lets you eat briskly, which matters: noodles keep softening in hot soup, and a bowl left to sit goes limp. Japanese even has a word for that sad fate, *nobiru* (伸びる), to stretch or go soggy.

You don't have to perform. A gentle slurp is plenty, and if you'd rather not slurp at all, nobody will mind. The point is that you don't need to wrestle the noodles into your mouth in silence.

A few other habits come with the territory:

- Use the *renge* (レンゲ), the flat-bottomed spoon, for broth, or as a little cradle for noodles on their way to your mouth.
- Lifting a bowl towards your mouth is normal in Japan; it's how people eat rice and miso soup. With ramen, plenty of people drink the last of the soup straight from the bowl, holding it in both hands.
- Eat while it's hot. A quick photo is fine; a long photoshoot while the noodles soften is the real faux pas. Some shops ask for no photos at all, so look for a sign.

I think the first slurp is the moment a bowl tells you what it is. It seems a shame to tiptoe past it.

## Chopsticks: two rules worth knowing

Most chopstick etiquette is forgiving, especially for visitors. Two rules are not, because both echo Buddhist funeral customs.

- **Don't stand your chopsticks upright in rice.** It recalls the bowl of rice set out for the dead, with chopsticks standing straight up in it. Rest them on the chopstick rest, or lay them neatly together.
- **Don't pass food from your chopsticks to someone else's.** After a cremation, family members pass the bones between chopsticks, and this is exactly what the gesture calls to mind. If you want to share, set the morsel on a small plate and let the other person pick it up.

Beyond those two, the usual courtesies apply: don't point with your chopsticks, don't wave them while you talk, and don't spear food with them. With disposable wooden chopsticks, *waribashi* (割り箸), some people avoid rubbing them together to remove splinters, since it can look like a comment that they're cheap. A quick glance and a gentle pick is fine.

## The words that bookend a meal

Two short phrases do a lot of work in Japan.

Before you eat, say *itadakimasu* (いただきます). It's often translated as "I humbly receive", and it carries thanks for the food and for everyone who brought it to you. Said quietly, to yourself or to the cook, it's perfect.

When you leave, say *gochisōsama deshita* (ごちそうさまでした), roughly "thank you for the meal". In a small ramen shop, the staff will often call back their thanks as you go. It's one of the nicest little exchanges in the country, and it costs nothing.

Some places hand you an *oshibori* (おしぼり), a damp towel, when you sit down. It's for your hands. You may see people mop their faces with it, but hands only is the safe choice, especially somewhere smart. Fold it and set it aside when you're done.

And then the thing that puzzles many visitors most: **there's no tipping in Japan.** Good service is simply part of the meal. Leaving coins on the counter is more likely to cause confusion than delight, and staff may well come after you to return what they assume is forgotten change. Pay what the ticket or the bill says, and say thank you.

## When the queue is waiting

Ramen shops are small, and the popular ones often have a line outside. That changes the rhythm of the meal.

- **Eat, then go.** A ramen shop isn't a café. When people are waiting, finishing your bowl and leaving promptly is the courteous thing to do; save long chats and phone scrolling for later.
- **Don't save seats.** Many shops will only seat a group once everyone has arrived.
- **Follow the shop's system.** Many shops have you buy a ticket from a machine first, sometimes before you join the queue. Our guide to the [ticket machine](/journal/ticket-machine/) walks you through it.
- **Leave the counter tidy.** In many counter shops, regulars lift their empty bowl onto the raised ledge and give their spot a wipe. It's a small kindness to the staff.

As for the broth: it's fine to leave some. Ramen soup is rich and salty, and plenty of people stop when they've had enough, with their sodium intake in mind. Finishing every drop can be taken as a compliment to the cook, but leaving a little in the bowl offends no one.

If you remember only one thing, make it this: a ramen shop is a small, busy room built around hot food. Slurp, say thank you, and hand the next person your stool while it's still warm.
