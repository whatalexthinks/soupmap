The first thing many ramen shops ask of you isn't a greeting or a menu. It's a machine. Slide open the door and there it is: a glowing panel of buttons, a slot for notes, a slot for coins, and quite possibly a line of hungry people behind you, waiting for you to make up your mind.

It looks more daunting than it is. Once you've used one, I suspect you'll wish every restaurant worked this way.

## Why the machine comes first

The *shokkenki* (食券機), or meal-ticket machine, is a fixture of ramen shops, standing soba counters and casual set-meal restaurants across Japan. You choose and pay up front, the machine prints a small ticket, a *shokken* (食券), and that ticket is your order.

For a small shop, it makes a lot of sense. The cook doesn't have to stop between bowls to take money or count change, orders are clear, and the line keeps moving. For a visitor, there's a hidden bonus: you can order a complete meal without speaking a word of Japanese.

The golden rule is simple: **buy your ticket before you sit down.** If you walk straight to a stool, staff will usually point you, kindly, back to the machine. When there's a queue, some shops have you buy first and collect tickets while you wait, so watch what the people ahead of you do.

## Reading the buttons

A typical machine is a grid of buttons, each with a dish name and its price, and sometimes a photo. Some newer machines have touch screens with an English option; many older ones are Japanese only.

A few patterns help:

- **The top-left button is often the house signature bowl.** It's a common habit rather than a rule, but if you're lost, it's a reasonable first guess. Bigger buttons, or ones in a different colour, tend to be the shop's pride too.
- **Bowls come first, extras come after.** Main dishes usually sit at the top; toppings, sides and drinks fill the rows below.
- **Sold out is marked.** An item that has run out usually shows 売切 (*urikire*) on or beside its button.
- **Buttons light up when you've paid enough.** Insert your money first, and the choices you can afford will glow.

If the characters mean nothing to you, a camera translation app does a decent job. Or match the kanji against our [ramen menu glossary](/journal/ramen-menu-glossary/), which covers the words you'll see most. And there's no shame in asking the staff *osusume wa?* (おすすめは？), "what do you recommend?"

## Toppings, extras and second tickets

Each button usually prints its own ticket, so a meal with extras means a little stack of paper. The machine keeps your balance as you press, so you can insert money once and make several choices.

The extras you'll meet most often:

- *Oomori* (大盛り): a large serving of noodles. The regular size is *nami* (並).
- *Ajitama* (味玉): a soft-boiled egg marinated in a soy-based sauce, with a jammy yolk.
- *Chāshū* (チャーシュー): extra slices of braised or roast pork.
- *Kaedama* (替え玉): a refill of noodles for your leftover broth, typical of Hakata-style tonkotsu shops. Depending on the shop, you may buy it as a ticket or call out for it and pay at the counter.
- *Raisu* (ライス): a bowl of rice, for a broth-soaked finish.
- *Setto* (セット): a set, often ramen with rice or *gyōza* (餃子).

When you've finished choosing, press *otsuri* (おつり) for your change. It's easy to forget, and otherwise your leftover balance just sits there for the next customer to spend.

## Paying and handing over the ticket

Cash still rules at many ramen shops. Some older machines only take coins and smaller notes, so it's worth breaking large notes before you go out to eat. Newer machines increasingly accept transport IC cards such as Suica, or QR-code payment apps, usually through a reader beside the buttons. Look for the logos before you count on it.

With ticket in hand, find a seat, or wait to be shown one. Then either hand the ticket to the staff or put it on the counter in front of you, where they'll collect it. Both are normal, and staff will gesture if they want it a particular way.

This is the moment you may be asked a question, usually about the noodles. Many shops let you choose the firmness:

- *Katame* (かため): firm
- *Futsū* (ふつう): regular
- *Yawarakame* (やわらかめ): soft

Some styles, such as the [iekei](/journal/iekei-yokohama/) shops of Yokohama, also let you choose the strength of the seasoning and the amount of fat. If you're unsure, *futsū* is always a safe answer. It's the bowl as the cook means it to be.

## Words to keep in your pocket

- 食券 (*shokken*): meal ticket
- 券売機 (*kenbaiki*): ticket machine
- 現金のみ (*genkin nomi*): cash only
- 交通系IC (*kōtsūkei IC*): transport IC cards
- 売切 (*urikire*): sold out
- おつり (*otsuri*): change
- 並 (*nami*) and 大盛り (*oomori*): regular and large
- トッピング (*toppingu*): toppings
- おすすめ (*osusume*): recommended

There's something quietly lovely about the system once it clicks. You decide, you pay, you sit, and the next thing anyone hands you is a bowl.
