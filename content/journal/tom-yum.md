Some soups soothe you. Tom yum wakes you up. The first spoonful is sour, then hot, then suddenly fragrant, as if someone has thrown open a window in a stuffy room. By the third spoonful your nose is running a little and you have stopped minding.

It is Thailand's most famous soup, and I think it earns that fame by being so alive. Nothing in the bowl is muted. Everything is turned up and somehow still in balance.

## What the name means

In Thai, *tom* refers to boiling, and *yum* (also spelled *yam*) is a way of mixing sharp, sour and spicy flavours, the same word that names a whole family of Thai salads. So tom yum is, more or less, a boiled dish dressed with that bright, mixed flavour.

The best-known version is *tom yum goong* (also written *kung*), made with prawns. Japanese menus usually write the soup as トムヤムクン (*tomuyamukun*), with the prawns built into the name. You will also find it made with chicken, fish, squid or mixed seafood, and mushrooms of some kind are a common addition whatever the protein.

## The aromatics do the heavy lifting

Unlike a broth that simmers through the night, tom yum can come together quickly. The base is often a light stock, and when prawns are involved, cooks commonly use their heads and shells to give it sweetness and colour. The character of the soup, though, comes from what is bruised and dropped into the pot:

- **Lemongrass**, cut into lengths and bashed to release its citrusy oils.
- **Galangal**, a relative of ginger with a sharper, piney, almost medicinal edge.
- **Makrut lime leaves**, torn so their perfume escapes.
- **Fresh chillies**, crushed or sliced, for heat that builds as you eat.

Then comes the seasoning, which is where the tension lives. Fish sauce brings salt and depth. Lime juice brings the sourness, and it is usually added at the very end, often off the heat, because boiling dulls its brightness. A good tom yum keeps all of these pulling against each other, so no single flavour wins.

## Clear or creamy

Tom yum comes in two broad forms. *Nam sai*, roughly "clear water", is a translucent broth, sharp and clean, where you can see every slice of galangal floating in it.

*Nam khon*, roughly "thick water", is the creamy version. It is made richer with evaporated milk or coconut milk, and often with a spoonful of roasted chilli paste (*nam prik pao*), which adds a little sweetness, a toasty depth and a warm orange-red colour.

Which is better? I would say clear for heat and focus, creamy for comfort. The clear version feels like a sprint; the creamy one lets you linger. Neither is a lesser version of the other; they are simply different moods.

## Tom kha, the gentle cousin

If you have ever been confused by creamy tom yum and *tom kha*, you are in good company. They share many aromatics, but tom kha is built around coconut milk from the start, and the soup is named for galangal itself, *kha* in Thai. The most famous version is *tom kha gai*, with chicken.

Tom kha is milder, rounder and sweeter: still sour, still fragrant, but less fiery. If tom yum is a shout, tom kha is a warm conversation. When I want the flavours of Thailand without the full blaze, that is the one I would point you towards.

## How to eat it

- **Think of it as part of a meal.** In Thailand, soups like tom yum are usually shared among several dishes and eaten with rice, rather than served as a solo bowl. Spooning a little over rice is a pleasure of its own.
- **Leave the woody bits.** Lemongrass stalks, galangal slices and lime leaves are there for flavour, not for chewing. It is perfectly normal to push them to the side.
- **Reach for the spoon.** Thai meals are generally eaten with a spoon and fork. Chopsticks tend to come out for noodle dishes, and tom yum noodle soups do exist if you want the best of both.
- **Ask about the heat.** Spice levels vary widely. There is no shame in asking for it milder.

Tom yum has also become a popular flavour for [instant noodles](/journal/instant-ramen/), which tells you how well its personality survives being shrunk into a sachet. And if you like the sour side of it, the tamarind-sharp [asam laksa](/journal/laksa/) of Penang is well worth a detour.

What stays with me is how little time tom yum needs to taste this deep. It proves that depth does not always come from hours on the stove. Sometimes it comes from a handful of fresh, loud ingredients, each allowed to speak at once.
