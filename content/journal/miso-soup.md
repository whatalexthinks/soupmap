Ask people in Japan which food tastes most like home, and miso soup comes up again and again. Not a feast, not a festival dish, but the plain bowl of misoshiru (味噌汁) that arrives with rice at breakfast, beside a set lunch, or at the end of a long day. It is so everyday that it is easy to overlook, and that, I think, is exactly what makes it special.

It is also one of the easiest soups in the world to make well, as long as you respect a couple of rules.

## Two parts, one rule

Miso soup is built from two things. The first is [dashi](/journal/dashi/), a light stock usually made from kombu and katsuobushi or from dried sardines. The second is miso, the fermented soybean paste. The fillings, called gu (具), cook in the dashi first. The miso goes in last.

That order matters. Much of miso's appeal is its aroma, and that aroma is delicate. If the soup boils after the miso goes in, the fragrance fades and the flavour turns flat. So the classic method is to turn the heat down, dissolve the miso into the stock, and warm it until it is just about to bubble. Japanese cooks have a word for that moment, niebana (煮えばな), and it is the ideal time to serve.

To dissolve miso smoothly, people use a small mesh strainer dipped into the pot, or put a spoonful in a ladle with some broth and loosen it with chopsticks before stirring it in. Either way, the aim is no lumps.

How much miso? Usually less than you think, adjusted by taste, because every miso has its own saltiness and sweetness. For more on that, see our guide to [miso types](/journal/miso-types/).

## The classic combinations

Part of the joy of miso soup is how much it changes with what goes in. A few classics you will meet again and again:

- **Tofu and wakame**: soft cubes of tofu with silky wakame seaweed, perhaps the most familiar pairing of all.
- **Nameko**: small amber mushrooms with a naturally slippery coating that gives the soup a gentle, silky body. Lovely with a darker miso.
- **Asari clams**: little saltwater clams that open in the pot and add a sweet, briny depth. Their juices are almost a dashi of their own.
- **Shijimi clams**: tiny freshwater clams with a rich, mineral flavour. They are popularly thought of as kind to the liver, which has given them a reputation as a morning-after soup.
- **Daikon and aburaage**: soft, sweet white radish with strips of fried tofu that soak up the broth.

Beyond these, almost anything seasonal works: potato and onion, spinach, cabbage, mushrooms, sweet potato, a beaten egg swirled in at the end. When pork and a crowd of root vegetables join, it becomes a different soup altogether, the hearty [tonjiru](/journal/tonjiru/).

## One soup, three dishes

Miso soup is not only a dish; it is part of the structure of a Japanese meal. The traditional template is called ichiju sansai (一汁三菜), "one soup, three dishes": rice, a soup, a main dish and two smaller sides, usually with pickles alongside. Very often, that one soup is miso soup.

You can see this logic everywhere, from a home breakfast of rice, miso soup, grilled fish and pickles to the set meals called teishoku (定食) in restaurants. In many places, a bowl of miso soup simply comes with the rice, no ordering required. In a traditional table setting, the rice bowl sits on the left and the soup on the right, both close to you.

## How to drink it

Miso soup is usually served in a small bowl, often with a lid, and traditionally made of lacquerware. Lacquer is light, keeps the soup warm, and does not get too hot to hold. There is no spoon. You lift the bowl in one hand and sip directly from the rim, using your chopsticks to guide the tofu, seaweed or clams into your mouth.

A few practical tips:

- If there is a lid, take it off and set it upside down beside the bowl. If it sticks, a gentle squeeze of the bowl's sides usually releases it.
- Give the soup a quick stir with your chopsticks before sipping. Miso settles, and you want the whole flavour, not a cloudy bottom and a clear top.
- With clams, pick out the meat with your chopsticks and leave the empty shells in the bowl.
- Sipping and slurping a little is perfectly fine. Hot soup is meant to be enjoyed hot.

## The beauty of the ordinary

There is no single correct miso soup. Every household has its own preferred miso, its own favourite fillings, its own idea of how strong it should be. The soup shifts with the seasons, with what is in the fridge, with the mood of whoever is cooking.

I find that comforting. Some dishes are about getting one perfect version right. Miso soup is about returning to something simple, day after day, and finding that it is never quite the same bowl twice.
