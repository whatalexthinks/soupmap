By day, the riverside in Nakasu is just a pavement. Then, as the light goes, the stalls arrive. Carts are wheeled into place, awnings unfold, benches and stools appear, lanterns come on, and before long a stretch of street has become a row of tiny restaurants, each one glowing beside the water with steam rising from its pots.

These are Fukuoka's *yatai* (屋台), open-air food stalls, and I'd count them among the best reasons to visit the city.

## A city that builds its restaurants every evening

Yatai turn up elsewhere in Japan, especially around festivals, but Fukuoka is the city most closely associated with them. Here they are part of everyday nightlife rather than a once-a-year treat. Each stall is a compact kitchen on wheels with a short counter and a few seats around it. They set up in the evening, trade into the night, and are packed away again so the pavement is clear by day.

They gather in a few main areas:

- **Nakasu** (中洲), the island district between two rivers, where stalls line the riverbank and their lights reflect in the water. It's the view on most postcards.
- **Tenjin** (天神), the downtown shopping and business district, where stalls set up along the streets among the office towers.
- **Nagahama** (長浜), near the fish market, the area long linked with Nagahama ramen, a style of tonkotsu with very thin noodles.

Each area has its own mood, from the riverside postcard of Nakasu to the after-work feel of Tenjin. Walking from one to the next is a fine way to spend an evening.

## What's on the counter

Ramen is the star. Fukuoka is the home of Hakata-style tonkotsu, a cloudy, creamy pork-bone broth with thin, firm noodles, and a bowl at a yatai counter late at night is something of a local rite. Many stalls serve it, and if you finish your noodles with broth to spare, ask for *kaedama* (替え玉), a refill of noodles, a habit often credited to the ramen shops of Nagahama. Our guide to [Hakata tonkotsu](/journal/hakata-tonkotsu/) goes deeper.

But a yatai is more than a ramen stand, and many stalls serve a whole evening's worth of small dishes:

- **Oden** (おでん): daikon, eggs, fish cakes and more, simmered in a pot of broth. Our [oden guide](/journal/oden/) has the details.
- **Grilled skewers**: *yakitori* (焼き鳥) and other skewers, cooked over the grill and eaten hot.
- **Gyōza** (餃子): dumplings. Fukuoka is known for small, bite-sized ones.
- **Drinks**: cold beer, *shōchū* (焼酎) and other drinks to go with it all.

Some stalls stick to the classics; others put their own spin on things. That variety is part of the fun of walking the row before you choose.

## The rules of the bench

Yatai aren't a free-for-all. Fukuoka has its own city rules for yatai, which govern matters such as where stalls may stand and how they must be run. The stalls you see are a regulated, licensed part of city life.

The other rules are unwritten, and they come down to space. A yatai counter is small, and you'll be sitting elbow to elbow with strangers: office workers, couples, locals on their way home, other travellers. Conversation across the counter comes naturally, and the cook is often part of it. In a country where restaurants can be wonderfully quiet, I think this is one of the easiest places to fall into a chat with the person beside you.

## How to enjoy a yatai night

- **Carry cash.** Some stalls may take other payment, but cash is the safe bet.
- **Keep your group small.** Two or three people fit far more easily than a crowd. If you're a larger party, split up and compare notes later.
- **Order properly.** The usual courtesy is to order a drink and a couple of dishes, rather than sitting with a single small plate.
- **Don't hog the seats.** Seats are few and others may be waiting. Enjoy yourself, then make room. Plenty of people move between stalls over an evening, a dish or two at each.
- **Look at the menu, or ask.** Some stalls post their menus; if not, it's fine to ask what's on offer before you order.
- **Plan for the basics.** A stall has no restroom of its own, so know where the nearest public toilets are.
- **Watch the weather.** Stalls are outdoors, and rain or strong wind can keep some of them closed.

A yatai is a restaurant that exists only for an evening, built from a cart and packed away by morning. Perhaps that's why a bowl of ramen there feels so particular: you're eating in a place that, by daylight, won't be there at all.
