Most parts of Japan have a famous food. Kagawa went further and wrote its noodle into its name. The prefecture's tourism campaigns call it *Udon-ken* (うどん県), "Udon Prefecture", and as slogans go, I've rarely seen one so honest.

Kagawa is the smallest of Japan's 47 prefectures by area, tucked into the northeast corner of Shikoku, the smallest of the country's four main islands. For a place that size, it casts a very long, noodle-shaped shadow.

The noodles go by an older name. *Sanuki* (讃岐) was the province here before Japan's modern prefectures were drawn, and the word stuck to the dish. Today "Sanuki udon" is printed on packets and shop signs all over the country. But the idea of it, firm, glossy and generous, still belongs to Kagawa.

## Koshi: the word that matters

If you keep one Japanese word from this article, make it *koshi* (コシ). It describes the texture of a good noodle: firm as you bite, then springing back, a gentle resistance that makes you chew rather than just swallow.

It isn't hardness, exactly. A tough noodle fights you. A noodle with koshi pushes back politely and then gives way, and the pleasure lives in that small negotiation.

Sanuki udon is the style most people think of when they hear the word. The noodles are thick and smooth, with a sheen on the surface and a bounce that survives a trip through hot broth. I think that texture is the whole point of the bowl. Everything else is there to frame it.

The framing is usually light. A classic Kagawa broth is clear and pale gold, a *dashi* (出汁, stock) often built on *iriko* (いりこ), small dried sardines, alongside kombu. Our piece on [dashi](/journal/dashi/) explains how that kind of stock comes together.

## How a self-service shop works

Kagawa is known for *serufu* (セルフ) shops, self-service counters that run a little like a cafeteria. The details change from shop to shop, but the rhythm is usually the same, and it's friendlier than it looks from the back of the queue.

1. Order your noodles at the counter, usually by style and size (small, medium or large), and say whether you want them hot or cold.
2. In some shops you're handed the noodles and finish the job yourself: warm them in a strainer dipped into a pot of hot water, then add broth from a ladle or dispenser.
3. Slide your tray past the toppings. Tempura is the main event, and *chikuwa-ten* (ちくわ天), a tube of fish cake fried in batter, is a Kagawa classic. You may also find fried tofu, eggs and rice balls.
4. Pay at the register at the end, for whatever is on your tray.
5. Help yourself to the condiments, often chopped green onion, grated ginger and crunchy tempura bits, then find a seat and eat.
6. Return your tray when you're done. There's usually a clear place for it.

The trap, as I see it, is the tempura. It's all within reach, it all looks golden, and a light lunch can turn into a feast before you reach the till. Choose one piece, maybe two. The noodles are the star.

## Four bowls to know

Menus can run long, but these four styles cover most of what you'll meet.

- **Kake** (かけ): the simplest bowl, hot noodles in hot broth with a little green onion. If a shop's noodles are good, this is where you'll notice.
- **Bukkake** (ぶっかけ): instead of a full bowl of broth, a smaller amount of stronger, concentrated sauce is poured over the noodles. It comes hot or cold, and on a warm day it's my pick.
- **Kamatama** (釜玉): noodles lifted straight from the pot (*kama*) and tossed with a raw egg (*tama*, short for *tamago*) and a splash of soy sauce. The heat just starts to set the egg into a silky coating. It's often compared to carbonara, and I don't think that's far off.
- **Zaru** (ざる): cold noodles on a bamboo tray with a dipping sauce on the side. Cold water tightens the noodle, so if koshi is what you came for, zaru shows it off most clearly.

You'll often see *kama-age* (釜揚げ) too: noodles served in their own hot cooking water, with dipping sauce on the side. It's softer and more comforting, the bowl to order when you want warmth more than bite.

## The monk and the noodle

Every great food has an origin story, and Kagawa's is a good one. Local lore credits *Kūkai* (空海), the Buddhist monk also known as Kōbō Daishi, with bringing the art of udon-making back from China in the early ninth century.

The story attaches to him neatly. Kūkai was born in Sanuki, he really did study in Tang-dynasty China, and he is revered across Shikoku, where the long pilgrimage route of 88 temples is associated with him. Whether he carried noodle know-how home in his luggage is another matter. There's no solid evidence for it, and it's best enjoyed as legend.

The more down-to-earth explanation points to the land itself: a dry climate that suited wheat, salt made along the coast, soy sauce from nearby Shōdoshima island, and small fish from the Seto Inland Sea for the broth. Put those together and udon starts to look less like a gift from a saint and more like common sense.

I like having both stories. One gives the noodle a saint, the other a landscape. But in Kagawa, the argument that really counts is the one your teeth make on the first bite.
