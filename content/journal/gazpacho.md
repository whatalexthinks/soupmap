Most soups begin with heat: a pot, a flame, a long simmer. Gazpacho begins the other way round. Nothing is cooked. Ripe vegetables, good oil and a little vinegar are blended into something cold, bright and almost drinkable, and the whole point is that it cools you down.

If you have only met gazpacho as a sad, watery starter, I urge you to give it another chance. Made well, with tomatoes at their peak, it tastes like summer poured into a bowl.

## Born in the heat

Gazpacho comes from Andalusia, the southern region of Spain, where summers are long and fiercely hot. It is often described as a humble country food, the kind of thing made from bread, oil, garlic and water to sustain people through a long day in the heat.

That last detail matters, because the tomato is a relatively recent guest. Tomatoes and peppers came to Europe from the Americas, and food historians generally describe older forms of gazpacho as pounded mixtures of bread, garlic, olive oil, vinegar and water, without the red. The version we know today grew from that base once the newer ingredients settled into Spanish kitchens.

I find it charming that one of the most famous tomato dishes in the world was once a soup with no tomatoes in it. It also explains the bread. Gazpacho comes from a frugal tradition, where yesterday's loaf was never wasted, and soaking stale bread into a soup was a practical way to make it filling.

## What goes in

A classic Andalusian gazpacho brings together:

- **Ripe tomatoes**, the heart of it.
- **Cucumber**, for coolness.
- **Green pepper**, for a slightly bitter, grassy note.
- **Garlic**, usually just a little.
- **Olive oil**, generously.
- **Vinegar**, often sherry vinegar in Andalusia, for brightness.
- **Bread**, usually stale and soaked, to give body.

Everything is blended until smooth, often strained, seasoned with salt and chilled thoroughly. Some cooks keep it a little rustic, with a hint of texture; others push it through a sieve until it is like velvet. I do not think there is a wrong answer, only a question of how hot the day is. The olive oil, blended in, turns the red into a softer, creamy coral and gives the soup a silky texture.

It may be served in a bowl with small garnishes of finely diced cucumber, pepper, onion or croutons, or poured into a glass and drunk like a cold, savoury juice. I love that it can be either, depending on the day.

Because nothing is cooked, there is nowhere to hide. Gazpacho is only as good as the tomatoes that go into it, which is why I think it is best saved for high summer, when they are deeply red and full of flavour.

## Salmorejo and ajoblanco: the cousins

Gazpacho has family nearby, and two relatives are especially worth knowing.

**Salmorejo** comes from Córdoba. It is made from tomatoes, bread, garlic and olive oil, with more bread and oil than gazpacho, and usually without the cucumber and pepper. The result is thick, smooth and closer to a purée than a drink, rich enough to hold its toppings: chopped hard-boiled egg and little pieces of *jamón*, Spanish cured ham. It is eaten with a spoon, slowly.

**Ajoblanco** is white rather than red. It is a cold soup of almonds, garlic, bread, olive oil, vinegar and water, blended until creamy. It is often associated with Málaga and is traditionally served with grapes, whose sweetness plays beautifully against the sharp garlic. It is often described as a descendant of those older, pre-tomato gazpachos, which makes it a kind of living fossil you can eat.

## How to eat it

- **Cold, really cold.** Gazpacho should be properly chilled. Lukewarm, it loses all its charm.
- **Bowl or glass, both are right.** A glass suits a hot afternoon; a bowl with garnishes suits a sit-down meal.
- **Try the cousins.** If gazpacho is too light for you, salmorejo is richer. If you want something surprising, try ajoblanco.
- **Eat it when it is hot outside.** This is a seasonal soup at heart, and it makes the most sense when the sun is doing its worst.

In Japan, summer brings its own cold bowls, such as chilled [sōmen](/journal/somen-summer/) noodles dipped in a light, icy sauce, and there is a whole rhythm of [seasonal soups](/journal/seasons-of-soup/) to go with the calendar. Gazpacho belongs in that same spirit, wherever you are.

What it reminds me is that soup is a shape, not a temperature. It is something to spoon or sip, made to suit the weather. In a hot kitchen at the height of summer, the kindest thing a cook can do is turn the stove off.
