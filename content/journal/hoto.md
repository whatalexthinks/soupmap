Most noodle soups keep their noodles and broth apart until the last minute. The noodles are boiled in one pot, the soup simmers in another, and they meet in the bowl. *Hōtō* (ほうとう) skips the formalities. It puts everything into one pot and lets it all get acquainted.

The result is the signature dish of Yamanashi Prefecture, the mountainous region west of Tokyo that takes in the northern slopes of Mount Fuji. It's thick, sweet, savoury and very hot, and on a cold day I can't think of many bowls that feel more like being looked after.

## A stew that happens to contain noodles

Hōtō noodles are made from wheat flour, like udon, but they're cut thick, flat and wide, more like broad ribbons than cords. Instead of being boiled separately, they go straight into a pot of miso-based soup along with a heap of vegetables.

The classic vegetable is *kabocha* (かぼちゃ), Japanese pumpkin, whose dense orange flesh softens and partly melts into the broth, adding sweetness and body. Around it you'll often find daikon radish, carrot, taro, mushrooms, Chinese cabbage and green onion, and sometimes pork or chicken. It's frequently served bubbling in a heavy iron pot, straight from the stove.

The texture is the thing. The noodles are soft and chewy, slippery with starch, and the soup clings to every piece. It's much closer to a stew than to what most people imagine when they hear "noodle soup".

## Why the noodles go straight in the pot

When udon is boiled, the cooking water carries away much of the flour from the surface of the noodles, and that water is usually poured away. Hōtō keeps it. Because the noodles go into the soup raw, their dusting of flour and the starch they shed stay in the pot, thickening the broth into something rich and almost creamy.

Hōtō dough is also commonly made without the salt that goes into udon dough; the miso broth does the seasoning instead. If you're used to the firm, springy bite of [Sanuki udon](/journal/sanuki-udon/), this is a different idea of what a wheat noodle can be: not a bounce, but a melt.

There's a practical logic to all this. One pot, less water, less fuss, and everything nourishing ends up in the bowl. Yamanashi is a land of mountains and basins, and the dish is often explained as the kind of hearty, flour-based cooking that made sense in a region with limited rice paddies. Gunma Prefecture has a close cousin, *okkirikomi* (おっきりこみ), built on the same idea of wide noodles simmered with vegetables.

The broth leans on miso for its depth and saltiness. If you want to know more about the range of miso and how it behaves in a pot, our guide to [miso types](/journal/miso-types/) is a good place to start.

## The general and the sword

Yamanashi was once the province of Kai, and its most famous son is Takeda Shingen (1521–1573), the warlord who ruled it during Japan's age of civil war. Local lore links him to hōtō: the story goes that he fed the dish to his troops as a hearty field meal, and some versions add that he cut the noodles with his own sword.

It's a wonderful image, and almost certainly better as legend than as history. There's no reliable record that settles it, and noodle origin stories have a way of attaching themselves to famous names. The word itself is usually traced to an old term for a flour dish that came from China, written with the rare characters 餺飥.

I like the legend anyway. A samurai feeding an army on pumpkin noodle stew says something true about the dish, even if it never happened: this is food for people with a long, cold day ahead of them.

## How to eat it

- **Expect a full meal.** A pot of hōtō is filling on its own. Come hungry.
- **Give it a moment.** It often arrives bubbling and stays hot for a long time, thanks to the thick broth and the heavy pot. Blow, wait, then dive in.
- **Mind the kabocha.** Eat it early if you like it in chunks, or let it slump into the soup for a sweeter, thicker finish. I'm firmly in the second camp.
- **Pick your season.** I think hōtō is at its best in autumn and winter, when the mountain air around Fuji turns sharp.
- **Look for variations.** You may come across versions with extra meat, or sweet local dishes that pair similar flat noodles with red beans.

To find a pot near you, [search the map for hōtō](/?q=hoto).

Hōtō isn't elegant, and it doesn't try to be. It's the kind of dish that makes sense of a landscape: cold air, steep slopes and one big pot that has to feed everyone at the table.
