For most of its life in Japan, ramen has been the opposite of fine dining. You eat it on a stool, often at a counter, sometimes after buying a ticket from a machine, and you're back on the street before your coat has warmed up. Nobody brings you a wine list. So when the news broke in late 2015 that a ramen shop had been awarded a Michelin star, it felt to many food lovers as if the wider world had finally noticed something ramen fans had always known.

## Michelin comes to Tokyo

The Michelin Guide began in France as a motoring guide published by the tyre company, and its stars became the most famous shorthand in fine dining. One star signals high-quality cooking that the inspectors consider worth a stop; two and three stars go further still.

Michelin published its first Tokyo guide in late 2007, and the city was showered with stars from the start, mostly for sushi counters, kaiseki restaurants and French dining rooms. Ramen, for years, was not part of that conversation.

That began to change with the Bib Gourmand, Michelin's list of restaurants offering good food at moderate prices. Once the Tokyo guide included Bib Gourmand selections, ramen shops started appearing on it. It was recognition, but of a particular kind: ramen was praised as great value rather than as a destination in its own right.

## Tsuta, Sugamo, 2015

Then came the 2016 edition of the Tokyo guide, announced at the end of 2015. Among its one-star restaurants was Tsuta (蔦, "ivy"), a small ramen shop in Sugamo, a neighbourhood in northern Tokyo better known for a shopping street so popular with older shoppers that it's affectionately nicknamed "Harajuku for grandmas". It was the first time a ramen shop anywhere had received a Michelin star.

Tsuta called its bowls soba, in the old Tokyo habit of calling ramen *chuka soba* (中華そば), and its signature was a shoyu ramen, widely reported at the time for a soy-based broth finished with a touch of truffle. It's a bowl rooted in the [Tokyo shoyu](/journal/tokyo-shoyu-chuka-soba/) tradition, refined with an obsessive eye for ingredients.

The reaction was immediate. The queue, already long, became famous, and the shop turned into a stop on many visitors' Tokyo itineraries.

A word of caution, though: guide ratings change from year to year. For any particular shop, check the current edition of the guide rather than any article, including this one.

## What a star did for ramen

It's hard to overstate how much attention the star brought. Headlines around the world told readers that a bowl of noodles had joined the ranks of haute cuisine. For ramen cooks who had spent years refining broths, sourcing soy sauce and flour, and making their own noodles, it was a public acknowledgement that what they did was craft, not just fast food. And it was a remarkable moment for a dish whose [history](/journal/ramen-history/) runs through port-town noodle shops and post-war street stalls.

It also fitted a wider shift in what the guide was willing to celebrate. Around the same time, Michelin's first Singapore guide gave stars to a couple of humble hawker stalls. In later editions of the Tokyo guide, more ramen shops received stars, and the Bib Gourmand list kept growing into a kind of map of affordable excellence.

I can see the case against the fuss, too. Part of ramen's magic is its ordinariness: it belongs to everyone, it's quick, and nobody needs permission to love it. Stars bring longer lines, higher expectations and a quiet suggestion that only certain bowls really count.

My own view is that both things are true. The star didn't change what ramen is. It changed who was paying attention.

## If you go chasing stars

If a starred or Bib Gourmand ramen shop is on your list, a few things help:

- **Check the current guide.** Stars and listings are reassessed for each edition, so don't rely on an old list.
- **Expect a queue.** Famous shops can draw long lines, and some use numbered tickets or reservation systems to manage demand. Look up how a shop handles it before you go.
- **Know the routine.** Many ramen shops, starred or not, take your order through a [ticket machine](/journal/ticket-machine/) at the door. Have your choice in mind before you reach the front.
- **Eat promptly.** Ramen is at its best in the first few minutes, and in a small shop with a line outside, lingering after you've finished is considered poor form.
- **Keep the rest of the map open.** Inspectors can only visit so many places, and some of the most memorable bowls in the country come from shops no guide has ever listed.

That last point matters most to me. A star didn't make ramen serious; the cooks were serious long before any inspector arrived. What it did was make the rest of the world look up from its plate and notice.
