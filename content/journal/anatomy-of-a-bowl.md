Watch a ramen cook at work and you will see a little ritual repeat itself all day. An empty bowl goes down. A small ladle of dark liquid goes into the bottom. Then a spoonful of fat, a long pour of steaming stock, a quick stir, the noodles lifted from the boiler with a sharp shake, and finally the toppings, arranged in seconds. Every bowl, every time, in much the same order.

That routine is the whole recipe in miniature. Ramen looks like one thing, but it is really five things working together: tare, stock, oil, noodles and toppings. Once you can tell them apart, you can taste any bowl and understand why it works.

## Tare: the bowl's real flavour

The tare (タレ) is a concentrated seasoning, and it is the part most visitors never notice. Only a few spoonfuls go into each bowl, yet it does most of the seasoning and decides the bowl's flavour category.

When a menu says shoyu, shio or miso, it is talking about the tare: a soy-sauce blend, a salt-based mix or a miso paste. Tare is rarely just one ingredient. A shoyu tare might combine several soy sauces with mirin, sake, kombu or dried fish. A shio tare can be built from salt and seafood extracts. A miso tare is often a blend of several misos with garlic, ginger or sesame. These recipes are often a shop's most closely guarded secret.

Notice that tonkotsu is missing from that list. Tonkotsu describes the stock, not the seasoning, and most tonkotsu bowls are seasoned with a salt- or soy-based tare. There is a fuller tour in [the four ramen flavours](/journal/four-ramen-flavours/).

So why does the tare go in first? Partly convenience: the hot stock poured on top mixes it in one motion. But there is a bigger reason. Keeping the stock unseasoned in the pot means one stock can serve several flavours, each bowl can be seasoned precisely, and the big pot does not drift saltier as it reduces over a long service.

## Stock: the body

The stock, usually just called the soup, gives the bowl its body and much of its aroma. The common foundations are pork bones, chicken carcasses, seafood such as dried sardines, kombu and katsuobushi, and aromatic vegetables like onion, garlic, ginger and the green tops of leeks. Plenty of shops combine an animal stock with a seafood one, sometimes simmered separately and blended to order.

The great divide is between clear and cloudy.

- **Chintan** (清湯), "clear soup", is simmered gently so the stock stays transparent. It tends to taste clean and layered, and it is the base of classic shoyu and shio bowls.
- **Paitan** (白湯), "white soup", is boiled hard for hours so fat, collagen and bone matter emulsify into a milky, creamy liquid. Pork paitan is what most people mean by tonkotsu, while chicken paitan, tori paitan, has its own devoted following.

## Oil and noodles

The glossy layer floating on top is no accident. Many shops add a measured spoonful of aroma oil, kōmiyu (香味油), to each bowl. It carries fragrance to your nose before the first sip, coats the noodles, and helps hold heat in the soup.

Common choices include chicken fat, golden and gently sweet; lard or pork back fat, rich and mellow; garlic oil, including the blackened garlic oil called mayu (マー油) that is famous in [Kumamoto ramen](/journal/kumamoto-ramen/); and scallion oil, made by frying green onion until fragrant. Two bowls with the same tare and stock can taste completely different with a different oil.

Then the noodles. Ramen noodles are wheat noodles made with kansui (かん水), an alkaline solution that gives them their springy bite, yellowish colour and distinctive aroma. Our article on [kansui noodles](/journal/kansui-noodles/) goes deeper into how it works.

Within that, the range is huge. Hakata's noodles are very thin, straight and served firm. Sapporo's are thicker, curly and chewy, sturdy enough for a heavy miso soup. Kitakata's are broad, flat and wavy. Curls catch more soup, thin noodles cook fast and soften quickly, and thick ones stand up to rich broths. In some styles you can even choose how firm you want them.

The pairing is where a cook's judgement shows. A delicate chintan can be drowned by a heavy noodle, and a dense paitan can overwhelm a fine one.

## Toppings: the finish

Last come the toppings: chashu pork, a soy-marinated egg, bamboo shoots, nori, green onion and many more. They add texture, contrast and a final flourish, and some are expected by style, like the spinach on an iekei bowl or the butter and corn on a Sapporo one. Our [ramen toppings glossary](/journal/ramen-toppings/) will help you decode a menu.

Toppings are also where the eye is drawn first, which is a little unfair. I think of them as the punctuation rather than the sentence. A beautifully arranged bowl on a weak soup is still a weak bowl.

## How to taste a bowl like a cook

Next time a bowl lands in front of you, take it apart in your head. Sip the soup first and ask what the stock is: pork, chicken, fish, or a mix? Then notice the seasoning: is it soy-dark, salt-clear, miso-nutty? Breathe in before the next sip and see if you can catch the oil. Then the noodles: how thick, how curly, how firm? Finally, which toppings are pulling their weight?

I think this is the most rewarding way to eat ramen. A great bowl is rarely great because of one perfect element. It is great because five simple parts, poured into a bowl in the right order, agree with each other.
