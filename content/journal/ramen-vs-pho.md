Put a bowl of ramen next to a bowl of phở and a stranger might call them the same thing: noodles, hot broth, meat on top, a cloud of steam. Taste them one after the other and you will never confuse them again.

They are often mentioned in the same breath, and I understand why. Both are fast, satisfying, everyday noodle soups that have travelled far beyond home. But they are built on completely different ideas. Think of them as cousins who share a grandparent, not twins.

## The noodle decides everything

Start at the bottom of the bowl, because that is where the difference begins.

Ramen noodles are made from wheat flour, and what makes them ramen is *kansui* (かん水), an alkaline mineral water mixed into the dough. It gives the noodles their springy, slightly firm bite, their yellowish tint and a faint, distinctive aroma. Depending on the style, they range from thin and straight to thick and wavy, and shops choose them to match their broth.

Phở uses *bánh phở*, flat rice noodles. They are white, soft and slippery, with a gentle chew rather than a spring. They do not fight the broth; they slide through it and carry it to you. Where a ramen noodle has a texture you notice, a phở noodle is more like a silk ribbon that keeps the soup company.

## Two ways to build a broth

Here is the most interesting difference, I think.

A bowl of ramen is assembled from separate parts at the last moment. There is the stock, made from pork, chicken, seafood or a mixture. There is the *tare*, a concentrated seasoning such as soy sauce, salt or miso, spooned into the empty bowl first. And there is an aroma oil, perhaps lard, chicken fat or a flavoured oil, floating on top. The cook combines them as the bowl is served, and the tare largely decides the bowl's identity. That is why ramen is so often described by flavour, as in the [four ramen flavours](/journal/four-ramen-flavours/), and why the [anatomy of a bowl](/journal/anatomy-of-a-bowl/) matters so much to ramen fans.

Phở does it the other way. The broth is a single, finished thing: beef bones (or chicken) simmered for hours with charred onion and ginger and whole spices such as star anise, cinnamon and cloves, seasoned with fish sauce in the pot. It arrives already complete. Its character comes from the bones and the spice, not from a seasoning added at the end.

Ramen broth can be clear or deeply opaque, light or heavy with fat. Phở broth is almost always clear, and it aims for fragrance and balance rather than richness.

## Toppings and table manners

Ramen toppings tend to be cooked and arranged in advance: slices of braised or roasted pork (*chāshū*), a soft-boiled egg, bamboo shoots (*menma*), a sheet of seaweed (*nori*) and chopped spring onion. The bowl usually arrives finished, with perhaps pepper, garlic or chilli oil on the counter for small adjustments.

Phở is more of a collaboration. Thin slices of raw beef often go on top and cook in the hot broth in front of you, alongside brisket or beef balls. In the southern style, a plate of fresh herbs, bean sprouts, lime and chilli comes on the side, with hoisin and chilli sauce on the table, and you build the bowl yourself. I wrote more about that in the [phở guide](/journal/pho/).

Some things they share. Both are eaten with chopsticks and a spoon. Both are meant to be eaten while hot, before the noodles soften. And slurping is perfectly fine with either, so do not be shy.

## Shared roots, separate lives

Both soups owe something to Chinese noodle traditions. Ramen began in Japan as a Chinese-style noodle soup, which is why it was known in its early days by names like *shina soba* and *chūka soba*, both meaning roughly "Chinese soba". Over the twentieth century it became thoroughly Japanese, splitting into regional styles.

Phở's origins are more debated. It is usually placed in northern Vietnam in the early twentieth century, and many accounts see Chinese influences alongside French ones, especially in the use of beef. Either way, neither soup is a copy of the other. They grew up in different kitchens, with different grains, different climates and different ideas about what a bowl should do.

## Which to choose when

These are opinions, not rules, but here is how I would decide:

- **For breakfast:** phở. It is a morning dish by tradition, and its clear, gentle broth is a kind way to start a day.
- **On a cold night:** ramen, especially a rich miso or tonkotsu bowl. It is built to warm you through.
- **When it is hot outside:** phở, or a light shio ramen. Something clear and bright.
- **When you want to tinker:** phở, with its herbs and sauces.
- **When you want the cook's final word:** ramen. The bowl arrives exactly as someone intended.
- **When you cannot decide:** have one now and the other tomorrow. Both deserve the full attention of an empty stomach.

If ramen wins today, our [map](/?q=ramen) is a good place to start looking. But I hope you will keep both in your life. Ramen and phở are not rivals. They are two answers to the same lovely question: what can a bowl of noodles and broth become, given enough care?
