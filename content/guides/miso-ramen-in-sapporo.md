Miso ramen is a young style by ramen standards. It is usually traced to Sapporo in the mid-1950s, with the shop Aji no Sanpei most often credited, and it spread across Japan in the 1960s as Sapporo ramen. What sets it apart isn't only the miso, the fermented soybean paste that seasons the broth. It's the wok: minced pork and vegetables hit very hot lard and sear before the stock is poured in, so the soup tastes of the pan as much as the pot.

Shops blend their own miso, often mixing darker, saltier red miso with milder white, and finish with garlic and ginger. Noodles are thick, curly and springy. Sweetcorn and a pat of butter are the classic add-ons, more popular with visitors than with every local. The places listed here are ones whose names, or our own notes, point to miso; plenty of other shops in the [Sapporo ramen guide](/guides/ramen-in-sapporo/) serve a miso bowl too. For the history, read [Sapporo miso: the bowl built for snow](/journal/sapporo-miso-ramen/); to cook it, try our [miso ramen recipe](/recipes/miso-ramen/).

## Know before you go
- Expect a richer, saltier bowl than shoyu or shio: a spoonful of the broth tells you how far to go.
- Learn the miso types in [Miso: white, red and everything between](/journal/miso-types/).
