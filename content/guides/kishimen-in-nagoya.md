Kishimen are wheat noodles rolled flat and cut into wide, thin ribbons. Because they're so thin they cook quickly and have a smooth, slippery texture, different from the chewy bite of Sanuki udon. In Nagoya they're usually served hot in a soy-and-dashi broth and topped with a generous pile of katsuobushi (bonito flakes) that wave in the steam, plus greens and often fried tofu or a fish cake. In summer, cold kishimen with a dipping sauce is common too.

The most famous place to eat them is perhaps the least romantic: the standing noodle counters on the platforms of Nagoya Station, where travellers grab a bowl between trains. Kishimen also appears on the menus of udon and soba shops across the city, so this guide is short; see [Udon in Nagoya](/guides/udon-in-nagoya/) for the wider list. Kishimen sometimes appears in the popular argument over Japan's three great udon. Read [Kishimen: Nagoya's ribbon noodle](/journal/kishimen/) and [Japan's three great udon, and the fight for third place](/journal/three-great-udon/).

## Know before you go
- Platform counters are quick and standing-only: order, eat, go.
- Stir the bonito flakes into the broth; they soften and season it.
