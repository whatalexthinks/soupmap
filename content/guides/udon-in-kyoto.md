Kyoto's udon belongs to the Kansai tradition: soft noodles in a light-coloured broth built on kombu and bonito, seasoned with light soy (usukuchi) so the dashi comes first. The city adds its own dishes. Kizami udon is topped with fried tofu cut into thin strips, rather than the sweet whole pocket of Osaka's kitsune. Ankake udon comes in a broth thickened with kudzu or potato starch, which keeps it hot, often with grated ginger. In Kyoto, ordering tanuki usually gets you that thickened broth with shredded fried tofu, quite different from Tokyo's tanuki with tempura scraps. Keiran udon, a thickened broth with beaten egg and ginger, is another local comfort.

Udon shops are everywhere, from neighbourhood counters to old houses near temples and the Gion district, and many also serve soba and rice bowls. For the related dishes, read about [Kansai dashi](/journal/dashi/), see [Soba in Kyoto](/guides/soba-in-kyoto/), or make a Kansai bowl at home with our [kitsune udon recipe](/recipes/kitsune-udon/).

## Know before you go
- Names change by city: in Kyoto, 'tanuki' and 'kitsune' don't always mean what they mean in Tokyo or Osaka.
- Thickened (ankake) broths stay very hot: let the first spoonful cool.
