For a city known for delicate cooking, Kyoto loves a heavy bowl. Its best-known contribution is kotteri ramen, a broth of chicken bones and vegetables cooked down until it's thick enough to coat a spoon, popularised by Tenka Ippin, a chain that began as a street stall in Kyoto in 1971. The city also keeps an older style: a dark, almost black shoyu broth with thin noodles and plenty of green onion (Kyoto's kujo negi), served at decades-old shops a short walk east of Kyoto Station, where two famous neighbours have drawn queues for generations.

In the north-east, the Ichijoji area near the universities became known as a ramen street, with many shops along one stretch of road competing for students. Kyoto Station itself has a ramen floor gathering shops from around Japan. The guide below covers the whole city, from Kawaramachi to Fushimi. If you like the creamy style, our [chicken paitan ramen recipe](/recipes/chicken-paitan-ramen/) makes a lighter cousin at home; for the city's other noodles, see [Udon in Kyoto](/guides/udon-in-kyoto/) and [Soba in Kyoto](/guides/soba-in-kyoto/).

## Know before you go
- 'Kotteri' means rich and thick; 'assari' means light. Many shops offer both.
- Kujo negi, Kyoto's green onion, is often free to add: look for a bowl on the counter.
